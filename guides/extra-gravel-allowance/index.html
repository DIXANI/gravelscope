How Much Extra Gravel Should You Order?

A small allowance can reduce the risk of running short, but the right margin depends on measurement quality, site conditions and how the material will be placed.

Planning guide Updated Oct 2026

Quick Answer: Extra Gravel Allowance by Scenario

The amount of extra gravel you should order depends on several factors. Here is a practical summary:

ScenarioRecommended AllowanceWhy
Simple rectangular area, flat ground5-10%Minor spillage and edge cleanup
Irregular shape, moderate complexity10-15%Measurement uncertainty and waste
Uneven or sloped ground10-15%Low spots require more material
Deep compaction required (driveways)10-20%Significant settlement during compaction
Uncertain measurements or rough subgrade15-20%Highest uncertainty factor

Key principle: Always calculate the base volume first, then add the allowance on top. Never guess a quantity and hope it is enough. A deliberate 5-10% add-on to a solid measurement is far more reliable than an optimistic estimate with no margin.

Why an Allowance Matters

Ordering exactly the calculated geometric volume assumes perfect measurements, a perfectly even site and no change between loose and placed material. Real projects rarely meet all three conditions. Even a carefully measured garden path will lose a small amount of material to spillage during delivery and spreading. A driveway layer will settle as it is compacted. An irregular shape is harder to measure precisely than a rectangle.

These losses are usually small in isolation, but they accumulate. A 2% spillage loss, a 5% compaction settlement and a 3% measurement uncertainty combine to create a gap between what you ordered and what actually ends up in the ground. Without an allowance, that gap means running short near the end of the job — which is when it matters most.

An allowance is simply a planning margin added after the base volume is calculated. It should be chosen deliberately rather than added automatically to every project. Understanding what each percentage point covers helps you pick the right number.

Where Gravel Is Lost or Changed

Before choosing an allowance, it helps to know exactly where material goes. The main sources of discrepancy between calculated and actual quantity are:

Spillage during delivery and spreading. When gravel is dumped from a truck or wheelbarrow, some material scatters outside the intended area. This is usually 1-3% for careful operators and more for rushed work.
Compaction and settlement. Loose aggregate contains air gaps between particles. As you compact the layer, those gaps shrink and the material settles. A 4-inch loose layer of compacted driveway gravel may finish at 3.2-3.6 inches. This is the single biggest source of quantity difference.
Uneven ground and low spots. Even when you level the sub-base, small depressions remain. Filling these bumps and hollows consumes more material than a flat-plane calculation predicts.
Edge waste and trimming. Cutting gravel to fit curved borders, tight corners or irregular edges creates off-cut material that cannot be reused elsewhere on the same project.
Measurement error. Tape measures stretch, laser meters bounce, and irregular boundaries are hard to quantify. A 5% error in length or width translates to roughly a 10% error in area.

Not every project experiences all five factors equally. A small rectangular flower bed on flat ground may only face spillage and minor measurement error. A large driveway on uneven soil faces compaction, settlement, low spots and measurement uncertainty all at once.

How to Choose the Right Percentage

The best approach is to match your allowance to the actual uncertainty in your project. Start with the base calculation, then evaluate each risk factor:

Use ranges, not false precision: Try the calculator at 0%, 5% and 10% allowance. Seeing how much the cubic yards and tons change helps you understand the financial impact of each percentage point and choose confidently.

Low uncertainty (5-10%): Flat ground, regular shape, confirmed measurements, shallow decorative layer, no heavy compaction. This covers most garden paths and borders.

Medium uncertainty (10-15%): Irregular shape, slight slope, moderate compaction, or measurements taken from a plan rather than on-site. This covers most patio bases and medium driveways.

High uncertainty (15-20%): Significant slope, poor sub-grade condition, deep structural layers, or measurements estimated from a photo or rough sketch. Only use 20% or more if you have verified that your base measurement is already reliable.

Do not use a large percentage to compensate for bad measurements. If you are unsure about the dimensions, measure again rather than ordering 20% extra. Over-ordering to cover uncertainty is a common mistake that wastes money and creates disposal problems.

Worked Example: 10x20 Driveway Section

Here is a complete example showing how the allowance changes the final order quantity:

  1. Area: 10 ft × 20 ft = 200 sq ft
  2. Depth: 6 inches = 0.5 ft
  3. Base volume: 200 × 0.5 = 100 cubic feet = 3.70 cubic yards
  4. Weight (general gravel, 2,700 lb/yd³): 3.70 × 2,700 = 10,000 lb ≈ 5.00 tons
  5. With 10% allowance: 3.70 × 1.10 = 4.07 cubic yards ≈ 5.50 tons
  6. With 15% allowance: 3.70 × 1.15 = 4.26 cubic yards ≈ 5.75 tons

In this example, the difference between 10% and 15% is about 0.25 tons or roughly 500 lb of additional gravel. That is about 10 extra 50lb bags. For a driveway project, that margin could be the difference between finishing smoothly and needing a second delivery.

The cost of that 0.25-ton difference is typically far less than the cost of a second delivery call-out, making the higher allowance the economically smarter choice for uncertain sites.

Balance Shortage Risk Against Leftover Material

Running short can mean paying for a second delivery, while over-ordering can leave material that must be stored or removed. The best allowance is therefore partly a logistics decision. Ask the supplier about minimum delivery quantities, whether small top-up loads are practical and whether unused bagged product can be returned.

For bulk deliveries, many suppliers charge a minimum order or a minimum delivery fee regardless of quantity. If your allowance pushes you just above a pricing tier, the extra material may effectively be free. Conversely, if you order significantly more than needed, you may face storage issues or disposal fees for the surplus.

Bagged gravel offers more flexibility for small projects. You can buy exact quantities and return unopened bags. But bagged material is usually more expensive per unit volume than bulk, so the allowance calculation works differently — buying one extra bag is simpler than arranging a partial bulk top-up.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding allowance before measuring. Always calculate the base volume first, then apply the percentage. Adding a round number upfront hides errors in the base calculation.
  • Using the same percentage for every project. A 10% allowance that is generous for a small garden path may be dangerously thin for a long driveway on uneven ground. Match the percentage to the project risks.
  • Ignoring compaction separately from waste. Compaction is a physical reality, not a wastage factor. If your finished depth matters, account for it by specifying a greater loose depth or by combining it with the waste allowance.
  • Ordering maximum allowance as a default. A 20% allowance on a well-measured project is wasteful. Reserve higher percentages for genuinely uncertain situations.
  • Forgetting that allowance increases weight too. More volume means more weight, which may affect delivery vehicle requirements or ground-bearing capacity on soft sites.

Common Questions

How much extra gravel should I order?

For a simple, well-measured area on firm ground, add 5-10% extra. For irregular shapes or uneven ground, 10-15% is safer. For deep compaction or uncertain measurements, 15-20% may be appropriate. Never add more than 20% without reconsidering your measurements first.

Does gravel compact when laid?

Yes. Loose gravel settles and compacts by approximately 10-20% depending on stone size and compaction method. A 4-inch loose layer may settle to 3.2-3.6 inches compacted. This is why deeper initial placement or a small overage is needed for projects requiring a specific finished depth.

Should I order more gravel for a driveway?

Driveways typically need 10-15% extra allowance. The deeper layers required for vehicle loads compact significantly, and driveways often cover larger areas where small measurement errors multiply. Always measure at multiple points across the driveway length and width.

Can I return unused gravel?

Bulk gravel returns depend on the supplier and local regulations. Some suppliers accept returns of clean, uncontaminated material within a short time window. Bagged gravel is usually returnable if unopened. Always ask before ordering large quantities with a high allowance.

What happens if I order too little gravel?

Running short means paying for a second delivery, which often costs more per unit than the original order. It also causes project delays and may result in visible seams or color differences if the second load comes from a different batch. It is almost always cheaper to order the correct amount with a modest allowance upfront.
