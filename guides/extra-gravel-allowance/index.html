How Much Extra Gravel Should You Order?

A small allowance can reduce the risk of running short, but the right margin depends on measurement quality, site conditions and how the material will be placed.

Planning guideUpdated Oct 2026

Why an allowance can be useful

Ordering exactly the calculated geometric volume assumes perfect measurements, a perfectly even site and no change between loose and placed material. Real projects often include small losses, uneven ground, settlement, compaction or minor changes at the edges.

An allowance is simply a planning margin added after the base volume is calculated. It should be chosen deliberately rather than added automatically to every project.

A practical way to choose the percentage

For a simple, well-measured decorative area on firm ground, a modest allowance may be enough. Irregular shapes, uncertain depth, deep ruts, significant compaction or a rough subgrade increase uncertainty. The less certain the measurements and final depth are, the more valuable it is to compare several scenarios before ordering.

Use ranges, not false precision: try the calculator at 0%, 5% and 10% allowance. Seeing the difference in cubic yards and tons is more useful than assuming one percentage is universally correct.

Do not use waste allowance to hide bad measurements

A large percentage cannot compensate reliably for a project that has not been measured. Break irregular areas into rectangles or circles, measure them separately and add the volumes together. For sloped or uneven sites, take several depth readings rather than using the maximum depth everywhere.

If a project includes a compacted structural base, the correct quantity may depend on specified compacted thickness and the aggregate's compaction characteristics. That is different from casually adding waste to a decorative layer.

Balance shortage risk against leftover material

Running short can mean paying for a second delivery, while over-ordering can leave material that must be stored or removed. The best allowance is therefore partly a logistics decision. Ask the supplier about minimum delivery quantities, whether small top-up loads are practical and whether unused bagged product can be returned.
