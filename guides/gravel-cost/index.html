How to Estimate Gravel Project Cost

Turn a gravel quantity estimate into a practical budget by separating material cost from delivery and site-preparation expenses.

Planning guideUpdated Oct 2026

Quick answer: gravel cost ranges by project type

A gravel project's total cost depends on four main factors: the area size, the depth you need, the aggregate type you select, and the delivery distance from the nearest supplier. The table below shows typical per-square-foot material costs for common project types, using average U.S. prices as a planning reference. These figures cover the gravel itself only and do not include site preparation, edging, or delivery charges.

Material cost breakdown: per ton, per cubic yard, per bag

Suppliers sell gravel in several different units, and the pricing model they use can change how you compare quotes. Understanding each unit helps you avoid mixing incompatible numbers.

Per ton (short ton = 2,000 lb): Most bulk aggregate suppliers price by the ton. Typical U.S. prices range from about $15 to $50 per ton for common crushed stone, depending on stone type and region. Specialty or decorative aggregates can exceed $100 per ton. When you know the weight you need, this is the most straightforward unit for bulk orders.

Per cubic yard: Some suppliers quote by the cubic yard, particularly for landscape stone or bagged-material resellers. One cubic yard of typical crushed stone weighs roughly 2,700 lb (about 1.35 short tons) on the loose, dry planning basis used by the calculator, so the per-cubic-yard price can be converted to a per-ton equivalent for comparison. Prices usually fall between $30 and $75 per cubic yard for standard aggregates.

Per bag: Bagged gravel is convenient for small projects but carries a significant premium. A typical 0.5 cubic foot bag of pea gravel or decorative stone weighs about 50 lb and costs $3 to $8 at a home center. In bulk terms, that is roughly $120 to $320 per cubic yard — often three to five times the bulk material price.

Budget tip: keep material cost, delivery and site-preparation costs as separate lines. That makes quotes easier to compare and prevents a low gravel price from hiding a high delivery charge.

Hidden costs most people forget

The gravel price you see on a quote is rarely the full cost of the project. Several common expenses are easy to overlook until they show up on the final invoice.

Delivery charges vary widely. Some suppliers include short-distance delivery in the per-ton price; others charge a flat fee, an hourly rate, or a per-mile surcharge. Minimum order quantities are also common — you may not be able to order less than one truckload even if your project is small. Remote or difficult-to-access sites often attract higher delivery fees.

Base preparation is another frequent surprise. If your existing ground is soft, weedy, or uneven, you may need to remove topsoil, add a compacted sub-base layer, or install geotextile fabric before laying the final gravel. These costs are entirely separate from the surface aggregate.

Edging and containment prevent gravel from spreading into lawns or garden beds over time. Options include metal or plastic edging, timber borders, or a dug trench. This is a small but important expense for long-term appearance.

Disposal and import costs apply when you need to remove existing material or bring in fill dirt. If your project involves significant excavation, factor in dump fees and hauling charges.

Equipment rental may be needed for larger jobs: a plate compactor for base layers, a skid steer for moving material, or a pickup truck with a tailgate lift gate if the delivery site is not truck-accessible.

Adding 15–30% to your material estimate for these indirect costs is a reasonable planning assumption for most residential projects.

Bagged vs. bulk: when each makes financial sense

The choice between bagged and bulk gravel is mainly a question of project scale and access. Bagged material is easy to buy, easy to transport in a personal vehicle, and easy to place in small amounts. Bulk delivery is far cheaper per unit of material but requires a truck to reach your site and typically a minimum order.

Bagged gravel makes sense when you need less than about one cubic yard — for example, filling a few small garden borders, topping up an existing path, or working in a tight backyard with no truck access. The convenience premium is worthwhile in these cases because the total spend stays modest.

Bulk gravel becomes financially superior as project size grows. Once you need more than two or three cubic yards, the per-unit savings of bulk delivery almost always outweigh the extra hassle of scheduling a drop. A full-load bulk order of crushed stone can cost as little as $15 to $25 per ton delivered, compared to the effective $120 to $320 per cubic yard of bagged material.

If you are torn between the two, consider ordering a small bulk load for the main area and filling in tight spots with a few bags. This hybrid approach keeps the total cost down while preserving flexibility for hard-to-reach corners.

Regional price variations: what affects cost in your area

Gravel prices vary significantly by region, and the difference is rarely just about the material itself. Several local factors drive the variation.

Proximity to a quarry or pit is the single biggest influence. Areas near active aggregate operations benefit from shorter haul distances and higher competition among suppliers. Rural regions close to quarries can see bulk crushed stone priced well below the national average, while remote areas without local sources pay a premium for long-haul delivery.

Local fuel costs and transportation regulations affect delivery pricing. States or regions with high diesel prices or strict weight-limit regulations will tend to have higher per-mile delivery charges. Urban areas may impose additional restrictions on truck access or delivery windows.

Aggregate type availability also matters. Common crushed limestone or granite is widely produced across much of the eastern and central United States and tends to be affordable. Exotic or imported decorative stones, or regions where the local geology produces only a limited range of aggregate sizes, will carry higher prices.

Seasonal demand can shift prices by 10–20% in some markets. Spring and early summer are peak seasons for landscaping and driveway projects, and suppliers may raise prices or impose minimum-order requirements during these periods. Ordering in late fall or winter can sometimes yield better rates, though weather and site conditions may limit your options.

To understand local pricing, call two or three nearby suppliers and ask for their current per-ton and per-cubic-yard rates for the aggregate type you need, including delivery to your address. Compare their quotes using the density conversion guidance so you are comparing like for like.

Cost-saving tips for gravel projects

There are several practical strategies that can reduce your gravel project cost without compromising the result.

1. Get multiple supplier quotes. Call at least three suppliers and ask for written estimates that include material, delivery, and any minimum-order fees. Quotes for the same product can differ by 20% or more depending on distance and current stock levels.

2. Time your order. If your project timeline is flexible, avoid the spring rush. Late fall or early winter orders often face less demand and may qualify for off-peak pricing. Just confirm that your site conditions can still support placement at that time of year.

3. Order the right amount to avoid double delivery. Running short means paying a second delivery fee, which can erase any savings from a tight initial estimate. Use the extra allowance guidance to choose a sensible margin above your calculated quantity rather than ordering exactly the geometric volume.

4. Reuse existing material when possible. If you are replacing an old gravel surface, the existing aggregate may still be serviceable. Screening and recompacting old gravel can save the full material cost, leaving only labor and any necessary new additions.

5. Choose the right aggregate for the job. A decorative polished stone looks impressive but costs more and may not be the best choice for a driveway. For structural or high-traffic applications, a locally sourced crushed stone is often cheaper, performs better, and is easier to maintain than a specialty import.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a cubic yard of gravel cost?
Across the United States, a cubic yard of common crushed stone typically costs between $30 and $75 for the material itself, excluding delivery. Specialty or decorative aggregates can cost $100 to $200 or more per cubic yard. The price you pay depends heavily on your region, the aggregate type, and whether the supplier charges separately for delivery.
Is it cheaper to buy gravel by the ton or by the cubic yard?
It depends on the supplier's pricing, not the unit itself. One cubic yard of crushed stone weighs roughly 2,700 lb (1.35 short tons) on the calculator's loose, dry planning basis, so you can convert between the two units using the material's bulk density. The real difference comes from how each supplier prices delivery and minimum orders. Always compare quotes on the same basis — either convert both to cost per ton or both to cost per cubic yard — before deciding.
How much does gravel delivery cost?
Delivery fees vary widely. Some suppliers include short-haul delivery in the material price; others charge a flat fee between $50 and $150, or an hourly rate plus mileage. Minimum order requirements are common — you may need to order at least one full truckload regardless of project size. Always ask whether the quoted price includes delivery and what the surcharge is for longer distances or difficult site access.
How much extra gravel should I order?
For a simple, well-measured project on firm ground, an extra 5–10% is usually sufficient to cover waste, settlement, and minor measurement uncertainty. For irregular shapes, uncertain depths, or sites with significant compaction requirements, consider 10–15%. The extra gravel allowance guide walks through how to choose the right margin for your specific situation.
Can I save money by buying bagged gravel instead of bulk?
Bagged gravel is rarely cheaper on a per-unit basis — it typically costs three to five times more per cubic yard than bulk material. However, bagged gravel can be the most practical and cost-effective option for very small projects where a bulk delivery minimum would force you to buy far more material than you need. If your project is under one cubic yard and you have no truck access, bags may actually save you money overall.
