Pea Gravel Calculator: How Much Pea Gravel Do You Need?

Pea gravel is sold by volume, weighed by the ton and often bought by the bag, and those three numbers only agree when they start from the same density. This guide shows the arithmetic behind a pea gravel calculator so you can check any quote or bag count yourself.

Planning guideUpdated Oct 2026

Quick answer: the pea gravel formula

A pea gravel calculator does three things in sequence. It turns your area and depth into a volume, converts that volume into weight using the material's density, then divides by bag size if you are buying in bags.

The formula: Volume (yd³) = length (ft) × width (ft) × depth (in) ÷ 324. Then Weight (short tons) = volume (yd³) × density (lb/yd³) ÷ 2,000.

The divisor of 324 comes from combining two conversions: depth in inches is divided by 12 to get feet, and cubic feet are divided by 27 to get cubic yards. 12 × 27 = 324, so the whole calculation collapses into a single step.

For pea gravel, GravelScope uses a planning density of 2,565 lb per cubic yard. That is the figure behind the calculator's pea gravel preset, and it matches the pea gravel row in the site's density reference, so the worked examples below and the live tool will give you the same answers. It is a planning assumption rather than a product specification: actual bulk density varies with the source rock, the grading and the moisture content, so a supplier's published density should take precedence. Substituting that figure into the calculator's custom density field is the single most useful refinement for a large order.

Two reciprocal figures follow from that density, and getting comfortable with them makes almost every pea gravel question easier. One cubic yard weighs about 1.28 short tons. And one short ton occupies about 0.78 cubic yards, because 2,000 ÷ 2,565 = 0.78.

Why pea gravel weighs less than crushed stone

Density is not a fixed property of the word "gravel". It depends on what the stone is made of, how it is graded, how much water sits between the particles and how much it has been compacted.

Pea gravel is rounded and usually sold in a narrow size range, commonly around 3–8 mm depending on the supplier. Because the particles are smooth and of similar size, they do not nest into one another the way angular crushed stone does: when nearly every particle is the same size, smaller particles cannot fill the gaps between the larger ones, so more of the volume is empty air. That is why the same cubic yard of pea gravel weighs less than a cubic yard of crushed stone — 2,565 versus 2,700 lb/yd³ in GravelScope's planning figures, roughly a 5% difference.

Every figure here is a bulk density: the weight of the stones plus the air between them, in the loose state in which material leaves the truck or the bag. It is not particle density, which describes the solid rock alone and is far higher. Bulk density is the correct basis for ordering material, and particle density should never be used for it.

Step-by-step: how to calculate pea gravel

Step 1 — Measure the area

Measure length and width in feet. For irregular shapes, split the area into rectangles, calculate each one, then add the results. A rectangle is length × width, and a circle is π × radius².

Step 2 — Choose a finished depth in inches

Pea gravel is normally laid at 2–3 inches for paths and light foot traffic, and 3–4 inches for patios and areas with regular use. Decide the depth before you calculate, because it is the single largest lever on the answer.

Step 3 — Convert to cubic yards

Volume (yd³) = length × width × depth ÷ 324. This combines the inches-to-feet and the cubic-feet-to-cubic-yards conversions into one divisor.

Step 4 — Decide whether you need an allowance

Add a percentage only if your project justifies it: uneven ground, edges that will settle, or a surface that will be topped up later. A 10% allowance is a common planning figure, not a standard.

Step 5 — Convert volume to weight

Weight (short tons) = volume (yd³) × 2,565 ÷ 2,000. Use your supplier's stated density instead of 2,565 if they publish one, because a product-specific figure beats a general planning estimate.

Step 6 — Convert to bags if needed

Bags = total weight (lb) ÷ weight per bag (lb), rounded up to the next whole bag. A 50 lb bag of pea gravel holds about 0.53 cubic feet at this density.

Each step matters because an error early on is multiplied by everything that follows. Depth is the usual culprit: a half-inch mistake on a large area changes the volume by roughly 20%.

Coverage: how much area does a ton of pea gravel cover?

This is the question that reverses the calculation. Instead of asking what a given area needs, ask how far a fixed quantity will spread.

At 2,565 lb per cubic yard, one short ton of pea gravel is 2,000 ÷ 2,565 = 0.78 cubic yards, which is about 21.05 cubic feet. Divide that volume by the depth to get an area.

The relationship is linear, which makes it easy to reason about: doubling the depth halves the area. At 2 inches — the most common depth for a decorative path — a single ton covers about 126 square feet, a strip roughly 3 feet wide and 42 feet long.

If you are working in metric, the same ton covers about 11.7 square metres at 2 inches. Note carefully that a US short ton is 2,000 lb while a metric tonne is 1,000 kg, or about 2,205 lb. A tonne is about 10% heavier, so it covers proportionally more area. Mixing the two units is a common and expensive error.

Worked examples

Three realistic scenarios, each calculated with the pea gravel density of 2,565 lb per cubic yard.

Example 1 — A 10 ft × 10 ft garden path at 2 inches

Area = 10 × 10 = 100 sq ft. Volume = 100 × 2 ÷ 324 = 0.62 cubic yards. Weight = 0.62 × 2,565 ÷ 2,000 = 0.79 short tons, which is about 1,583 lb. In 50 lb bags that is 1,583 ÷ 50 = 31.7, rounded up to 32 bags.

This is the case where bags and bulk are competitive, because the whole job is under a ton. Below roughly half a ton, bagged material can be the more practical choice if you have no easy way to receive a delivery.

Example 2 — A 12 ft × 20 ft patio at 3 inches, with a 10% allowance

Area = 12 × 20 = 240 sq ft. Base volume = 240 × 3 ÷ 324 = 2.22 cubic yards. With a 10% allowance the order volume becomes 2.22 × 1.10 = 2.44 cubic yards. Weight = 2.44 × 2,565 ÷ 2,000 = 3.14 short tons, or about 6,270 lb. That is 126 bags of 50 lb — a strong signal that this project should be bought in bulk.

The allowance matters here. Without it you would have ordered 2.22 cubic yards and about 2.85 short tons; the 10% adds roughly 0.29 tons. The same 10% applied to a much larger patio would add proportionally more material, so make that decision deliberately.

Example 3 — A 4 ft × 30 ft border at 2 inches, with a 10% allowance

Area = 4 × 30 = 120 sq ft. Base volume = 120 × 2 ÷ 324 = 0.74 cubic yards. With a 10% allowance: 0.81 cubic yards. Weight = 0.81 × 2,565 ÷ 2,000 = 1.05 short tons, about 2,090 lb, or 42 bags of 50 lb.

All three examples can be reproduced in the GravelScope gravel calculator by selecting the Pea gravel preset, entering the dimensions and depth, and setting the allowance to 0 or 10%. The calculator applies the same density and the same rounding, so the outputs should agree.

Bag size and bag counts

Bagged pea gravel is sold by weight, and the bag weight is different from the bag volume. A 50 lb bag of pea gravel at 2,565 lb/yd³ holds about 0.53 cubic feet. A 40 lb bag holds about 0.42 cubic feet.

Bag weightApproximate volume per bagBags per cubic yard
50 lb≈ 0.53 ft³≈ 51
40 lb≈ 0.42 ft³≈ 64
25 lb≈ 0.26 ft³≈ 103

Always round the bag count up. If the arithmetic gives 31.7 bags you need 32, because a supplier cannot sell you seven-tenths of a bag. The rounding is slight, but it is easy to arrive one or two bags short and have to make a second trip.

Keep the two methods separate. You can calculate bags from weight (total pounds ÷ bag pounds) or from volume (total cubic feet ÷ bag cubic feet). Both agree at a consistent density, but mixing a weight-based bag count with a volume-based quantity will usually drift. Use one or the other, and check that the bag count and the volume tell the same story.

Depth is the input most people get wrong. These are the usual planning ranges for pea gravel specifically.

Pea gravel is rounded, so it does not lock together the way angular crushed stone does. That makes it comfortable underfoot but also means it shifts: a very thin layer on soft ground will disappear into the soil below over a season or two. Ordering: how much extra pea gravel should you buy?

An allowance exists to cover specific, identifiable risks — not to round the order up out of caution.

  • Settlement and compaction — loose pea gravel settles, and a surface that is walked on or raked will lose some depth over time.
  • Uneven or sloped ground — a nominally flat area usually is not. Thin spots at the low end of a slope need extra material.
  • Edges and transitions — where gravel meets a lawn, a paving edge or a wall, some material is lost to raking and tidying.
  • Top-up — a decorative bed is often topped up in a later season as the surface weathers.

If none of these apply to you, a 0% allowance is a defensible choice, and the calculator accepts it. If several apply, 5–10% is the usual planning range. Do not stack allowances: applying 10% for settlement, then another 10% for waste, then 10% for access produces an order about 33% above the calculated volume rather than 10% above it. Choose one figure that reflects your project and apply it once. The extra gravel allowance guide works through the decision in more detail.

Mistakes that change the answer

  • Using the wrong density. Pea gravel at 2,565 lb/yd³ is lighter than crushed stone. Borrowing a crushed stone figure overstates the tonnage by about 5%, which matters on a large order.
  • Mixing short tons and tonnes. A metric tonne is about 2,205 lb, roughly 10% more than a US short ton. If your supplier quotes tonnes and you calculated short tons, the numbers will not match.
  • Confusing volume and weight. Cubic yards describe space; tons describe weight. A supplier selling by the yard and one selling by the ton are quoting genuinely different things, and the conversion depends on density.
  • Measuring depth after compaction. Calculate using the loose depth you will actually spread, since that is what you are buying. A finished depth of 2 inches needs slightly more loose material.
  • Applying an allowance twice. Decide once, apply once.
  • Forgetting that bag volume and bag weight differ. Check whether a bag's stated coverage is based on its weight or its volume, rather than assuming.
  • Assuming pea gravel for a driveway. Rounded stone migrates under vehicle weight; use angular crushed stone for a driveway surface.

Sources and further reading

The density and conversion logic in this guide is explained rather than invented, and the figures can be checked against the references below. Where a source states a range, the range is reported as published.

  • Mass, Weight, Density or Specific Gravity of Bulk Materials — bulk density reference for over 300 dry materials, whose nearest entries are "Sand with Gravel, dry" (103 lb/ft³) and "Sand, loose" (90 lb/ft³); it has no pea-gravel entry and is cited here only to bound the general range of loose bulk aggregate densities. The 2,565 lb/yd³ used in this guide is GravelScope's own planning figure, not a product specification.
  • Gravel — background on how gravel is defined and graded, including the distinction between natural gravel and manufactured crushed stone, and a widely cited order-of-magnitude figure of roughly 1,800 kg per cubic metre for loose gravel.
  • NIST Office of Weights and Measures — Unit Conversion — the authoritative approach to unit conversion and rounding, and the source for using exact conversion factors rather than approximations.
  • USGS National Minerals Information Center — Crushed Stone Statistics and Information — government statistics on crushed stone and construction sand and gravel supply in the United States, and context for how aggregate is classified and used.

Two limitations are worth stating plainly. First, no single density figure is correct for every pea gravel product, because the stone's origin and grading vary; the 2,565 lb/yd³ used here is a planning figure, and a supplier's stated density always overrides it. Second, these calculations describe material quantity, not structural design. Load-bearing, drainage and edge restraint decisions need an assessment that a volume calculation cannot provide.

Pea gravel calculator FAQ

How much does a cubic yard of pea gravel weigh?

At the planning density GravelScope uses for pea gravel, 2,565 lb per cubic yard, one cubic yard weighs about 1.28 short tons, or roughly 1,163 kg. Rounded, single-size pea gravel holds more void space than crushed stone, which is why it is lighter per cubic yard than most alternatives.

How many bags of pea gravel do I need for 100 square feet?

It depends on depth and bag size. At 2 inches deep, 100 square feet needs about 0.62 cubic yards, which is roughly 1,583 lb. That is about 32 bags of 50 lb, or 40 bags of 40 lb. Always round bag counts up to the next whole bag.

How deep should pea gravel be?

For paths and light foot traffic, 2 to 3 inches is the usual planning range. For patios and areas with regular use, 3 to 4 inches gives a more stable surface. Pea gravel is rounded, so it does not lock together the way angular crushed stone does, and a very thin layer can shift underfoot.

Can I use pea gravel for a driveway?

Pea gravel is normally a poor choice for a driveway surface. Because the stones are smooth and rounded, they roll rather than interlock and tend to migrate under vehicle weight. Angular crushed stone is the usual choice for driveways, with pea gravel reserved for paths, beds and decorative areas.

How do I convert pea gravel cubic yards to tons?

Multiply the cubic yards by the density in pounds per cubic yard, then divide by 2,000. Using the GravelScope pea gravel density of 2,565 lb per cubic yard: cubic yards × 2,565 ÷ 2,000 = short tons. For example, 2 cubic yards × 2,565 ÷ 2,000 = 2.57 short tons.

Does pea gravel need a weed barrier underneath?

A woven geotextile fabric is often used under decorative pea gravel to separate the stone from the soil below and reduce weeds working up through it. It will not stop weeds that germinate on top of the stone. Loose fabric can also make pea gravel feel less stable, so check the installation guidance for your project.
