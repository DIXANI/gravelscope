Gravel Density: Tons vs Cubic Yards

Volume tells you how much space the gravel fills. Density is what turns that volume into an estimated weight. Understanding both is essential for accurate ordering.

Volume and Weight Are Different Measurements

Cubic yards and cubic feet describe how much space the gravel occupies. Tons and kilograms describe weight. Suppliers may sell by either measure, which is why a project can be calculated correctly by volume but still need a density estimate before it can be converted to tons.

The basic relationship is simple: estimated weight equals volume multiplied by bulk density. The difficulty is that bulk density is not identical for every aggregate. Two piles of gravel that occupy exactly the same cubic yards can weigh noticeably different amounts if they are made from different stone types or if one is wetter than the other.

This is why GravelScope asks for density as part of its calculation. The preset materials cover the most common options, but entering a custom density whenever you have supplier data makes the weight estimate significantly more reliable. The volume calculation remains unchanged — only the weight conversion is affected.

Why Gravel Density Varies

Stone type, particle size, grading, moisture and the amount of empty space between particles all affect bulk density. These factors combine in ways that make a single universal density impossible.

Stone composition is the primary factor. Granite is denser than limestone, and basalt is denser than both. A cubic yard of granite gravel will always weigh more than a cubic yard of limestone gravel, even when both are perfectly dry and equally compacted. This is a fundamental property of the rock itself.

Particle size and grading also matter. Well-graded aggregate — a mix of large and small stones — packs more tightly than uniformly sized gravel because the smaller particles fill the voids between the larger ones. This reduces air space and increases weight per cubic yard. Uniformly sized pea gravel, by contrast, has more empty space between the rounded stones and therefore a lower bulk density.

Moisture content can add 5-15% to the weight of a given volume. Water fills the air spaces between particles and adds its own weight. Rain during delivery or storage in damp conditions can make a shipment heavier than expected, which is why weight-based ordering can appear inconsistent from one delivery to the next even when the volume is identical.

Example: Two products occupying the same 2 cubic yards but with different bulk densities will require different tonnage even though the project volume is unchanged. Always check which unit your supplier uses — volume or weight — before comparing quotes.

Converting Between Units

Being comfortable with the basic conversions prevents errors when suppliers use different units. Here are the essential relationships:

Cubic yards to cubic feet: Multiply by 27. One cubic yard always equals 27 cubic feet, regardless of material.
Cubic feet to pounds: Multiply by density in lb/ft³. For general gravel at 100 lb/ft³, 27 cubic feet = 2,700 lbs.
Pounds to short tons: Divide by 2,000. 2,700 lbs ÷ 2,000 = 1.35 tons.
Combined formula: Tons = (Cubic yards × 27 × density in lb/ft³) ÷ 2,000. Or simplified: Tons = Cubic yards × (density × 0.0135).

Worked example: You need 3 cubic yards of crushed stone with a density of 100 lb/ft³. Weight = 3 × 27 × 100 ÷ 2,000 = 4.05 tons. If your supplier quotes by the ton, you would order approximately 4.1 tons. If they quote by the cubic yard, you order 3 cubic yards and let them handle the weight conversion.

For metric users: 1 cubic meter of general gravel (about 1,600 kg/m³) weighs approximately 1.6 metric tonnes. The same formula applies — volume multiplied by density equals weight. Note that the calculator's presets are US customary (2,565–2,835 lb/yd³); in metric mode the tool displays the equivalent planning density in kg/m³ and you should substitute your supplier's figure when they provide one.

When Generic Density Fails

A generic calculator density is best treated as a planning estimate. There are several situations where you should substitute supplier-specific data instead:

  • Large orders over 10 tons. A 10% density error on a 10-ton order is 1 full ton — enough to change your delivery schedule and budget significantly.
  • Structural or engineering applications. Driveway bases, retaining wall backfill and foundation drainage layers may have engineering specifications that require exact density values.
  • Comparing supplier quotes. If one supplier quotes per ton and another per cubic yard, you need accurate density to make a fair comparison.
  • Weight-restricted sites. Soft ground, bridges, or residential streets with weight limits may restrict how much weight can be delivered at once.
  • Moisture-sensitive projects. Some applications require dry aggregate, and wet material will weigh more than expected, potentially exceeding your budget or delivery capacity.

If a quarry, landscape yard or supplier publishes a weight per cubic yard or bulk density for the exact product you plan to buy, use that figure. It eliminates the single largest source of estimation error in any gravel calculation.

Practical Calculator Tips

GravelScope handles density automatically for its preset materials, but entering a custom density is straightforward when you have better data:

  1. Run the calculator with the default preset for your material type to get a baseline estimate.
  2. Call your supplier and ask for the weight per cubic yard or the bulk density in lb/ft³ for the exact product you will receive.
  3. Switch to custom density in the calculator and enter the supplier's figure.
  4. Re-run the calculation — the volume stays the same, only the weight changes.
  5. Use the updated weight for your budget and delivery planning.

This two-step process takes only a minute and can prevent costly ordering mistakes. The volume calculation is always correct — it is the weight conversion that benefits from supplier data.

Common Questions

How many tons is 1 cubic yard of gravel?

One cubic yard of general gravel weighs approximately 1.4-1.6 tons (2,800-3,200 lbs) depending on the stone type and moisture content. Crushed stone is typically around 1.4 tons per cubic yard, while denser granite gravel can reach 1.7 tons per cubic yard.

Why do tons and cubic yards differ for the same gravel?

Tons measure weight while cubic yards measure volume. The same volume of different gravel types can weigh very differently because density varies with stone composition, particle size, and void spaces between grains. Wet gravel also weighs more than dry gravel due to water filling the air spaces.

What is the density of crushed stone per cubic yard?

Crushed stone typically has a bulk density of about 100 lb per cubic foot, which equals approximately 2,700 lb per cubic yard or 1.35 short tons per cubic yard. This can range from 90-110 lb/ft³ depending on the specific rock type and grading.

Should I use supplier density or generic estimates?

Always prefer supplier-provided density when available. Generic estimates like the 2,700 lb/yd³ general-gravel preset are useful for planning but can be off by 10-15% compared to the actual product. For large orders or structural applications, the supplier's published weight per cubic yard eliminates this uncertainty.

How do I convert cubic yards to tons manually?

Multiply cubic yards by the density in lb/yd³, then divide by 2,000 to get short tons. Formula: tons = (cubic yards × density) / 2000. For general gravel at 2,700 lb/yd³: 3 cubic yards × 2,700 ÷ 2,000 = 4.05 tons.
