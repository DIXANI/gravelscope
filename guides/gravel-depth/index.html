How Deep Should Gravel Be?

Gravel depth depends on what the area will carry, how the ground underneath behaves and whether the finished surface needs to stay stable through wet seasons and heavy use.

Planning guideUpdated Oct 2026

Quick Answer: Depth by Application

The correct gravel depth varies significantly by project type. Here are the most common planning ranges for compacted finished depth:

4-6 inches
ApplicationFinished DepthLoose Depth (before compact)Notes
Decorative border1-2 inches1.2-2.3 inchesLight foot traffic only
Garden path2-3 inches2.3-3.5 inchesWell-drained, stable base
Pedestrian patio3-4 inches3.5-4.5 inchesRegular foot traffic
Residential driveway4-6 inches4.5-7 inchesCars and light vehicles
Heavy driveway6-8 inches7-9 inchesTrucks, RVs, frequent use

Key principle: These are compacted finished depths. When ordering material, calculate using the loose depth, which is approximately 10-20% greater than the target finished depth to account for settlement during compaction.

Why Depth Matters More Than Quantity Alone

Two projects can use the same cubic yards of gravel but perform very differently if their depths differ. A thin layer of gravel on a soft subgrade will sink, ravel and separate under vehicle loads. A properly deep layer distributes load across a wider area of the underlying soil, reducing pressure on any single point.

Depth also affects drainage. Thinner layers in areas with poor natural drainage can become waterlogged, turning gravel into mud. A minimum thickness ensures the aggregate matrix remains above the capillary rise zone of the subsoil, preserving permeability and stability.

This is why depth planning usually comes before volume calculation. You choose the depth that your application requires, then use the calculator to turn that depth into the cubic yards or tons you need to order. Reversing that order — picking a quantity first and hoping the depth works out — produces uneven or structurally insufficient results.

Factors That Change the Right Depth

Beyond the basic application type, several site-specific factors push the correct depth higher or lower:

1
Traffic load and frequency. A path used only occasionally by pedestrians needs less depth than a driveway that receives daily vehicle traffic. Heavier loads require deeper aggregate to prevent stone displacement and rutting.
2
Subgrade condition. Soft clay, peat or freshly filled ground cannot support thin gravel layers. Poor subgrades often need a thicker structural base or geotextile reinforcement to prevent the aggregate from mixing into the underlying soil.
3
Drainage and water table. Areas that collect standing water or sit near the water table benefit from slightly deeper aggregate to maintain a stable riding surface above saturated ground.
4
Stone size and grading. Larger angular stones interlock better and can sometimes perform adequately at slightly reduced depths compared to rounded uniform gravel. Well-graded aggregates pack denser and resist movement better than single-size material.
5
Climate and freeze-thaw. In cold climates, frost heave can push shallow gravel upward and outward. A deeper layer provides more mass to resist seasonal movement and reduces the risk of heaving damage.

When any of these factors are present, the planning ranges in the quick-answer table should be treated as a minimum, not a target. It is safer to exceed the recommended depth slightly than to undershoot and discover the problem after the material is already in place.

Understanding Compaction and Settlement

One of the most important concepts in gravel planning is that loose depth and finished depth are not the same. When gravel is spread and compacted, the particles rearrange into a tighter configuration, reducing the overall thickness of the layer.

A typical compaction range is 10-20% settlement. This means:

  • A 5-inch loose layer compacts to approximately 4-4.5 inches finished depth
  • A 6-inch loose layer compacts to approximately 4.8-5.4 inches finished depth
  • A 4-inch loose layer compacts to approximately 3.2-3.6 inches finished depth

The exact settlement percentage depends on stone shape, size distribution, moisture content and the compaction method used. Angular crushed stone compacts more predictably than rounded pea gravel. Mechanical rolling achieves greater settlement than hand tamping.

Practical tip: If your project specification calls for a finished depth of 4 inches, plan to spread the gravel to approximately 4.5-5 inches loose. Use a straight board across stakes at the target depth as you spread, then compact and recheck.

Worked Example: Driveway Depth Planning

Here is a complete example showing how depth planning connects to the volume calculation:

  1. Project: 10 ft × 20 ft residential driveway
  2. Required finished depth: 4 inches (standard for light vehicle traffic)
  3. Compaction factor: 15% (typical for crushed stone)
  4. Loose depth needed: 4 ÷ 0.85 = 4.7 inches (round to 5 inches)
  5. Area: 10 × 20 = 200 sq ft
  6. Volume: 200 × (5/12) = 83.3 cubic feet = 3.09 cubic yards
  7. Add 10% allowance: 3.09 × 1.10 = 3.40 cubic yards
  8. Weight estimate (crushed stone at a loose, dry planning density of 100 lb/ft³ = 2,700 lb/yd³, the calculator's crushed-stone preset): 3.40 × 27 × 100 ÷ 2000 = 4.59 tons

This example shows that depth is the bridge between the physical project and the calculator result. Change the depth from 4 to 6 inches and the volume increases by 50%, directly affecting both the quantity ordered and the total cost.

Measuring Depth Accurately on Site

Accurate depth measurement during installation prevents both under-ordering and over-spreading. Here are practical methods:

  • Stake method: Drive stakes around the perimeter at the target loose depth. Use a string line or straight board stretched between stakes as a depth reference while spreading.
  • Ruler or tape check: Drop a ruler vertically into the loose gravel at several points. This gives an instant reading of current depth before compaction.
  • Compaction test strip: Spread and compact a small test area first. Measure the before-and-after thickness to determine the actual compaction ratio for your specific material, then apply that ratio to the full project.
  • Multiple check points: Ground is rarely perfectly flat. Check depth at 4-6 points across the area and adjust high spots by removing material or low spots by adding more.

Checking depth at multiple points is especially important for larger projects. A driveway that appears level from the street may have subtle dips that require additional material in specific zones.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Specifying finished depth instead of loose depth when ordering. Suppliers quote based on the material you receive, not what it becomes after compaction. Always calculate using loose depth.
  • Ignoring compaction in the calculation. Ordering exactly the volume needed for the finished depth without accounting for settlement leaves you short.
  • Using the same depth for all applications. A decorative garden bed does not need the same depth as a vehicle driveway. Matching depth to use prevents both waste and failure.
  • Overlooking subgrade variation. Soft spots or organic material under part of the area may require additional depth or preparation in those zones specifically.
  • Neglecting edge containment. Deeper gravel without proper edging tends to spread outward under traffic, reducing effective depth over time. Edging preserves the designed depth.

Common Questions

How deep should gravel be for a driveway?

For residential driveways, 4-6 inches of compacted gravel is standard. Heavy vehicles or frequent truck traffic may require 6-8 inches. Always include a compacted sub-base layer beneath the surface gravel for stability.

Is 2 inches of gravel enough for a pathway?

For light foot traffic on a stable, well-drained path, 2 inches of compacted gravel is acceptable. For heavier use or softer ground, increase to 3-4 inches to prevent the stones from working into the soil below.

Does gravel depth change when it compacts?

Yes. Loose gravel settles approximately 10-20% after compaction. A 4-inch loose layer typically compacts to 3.2-3.6 inches. Always specify loose depth when ordering material, then account for settlement to achieve your target finished depth.

How do I measure gravel depth accurately?

Use a ruler or measuring tape dropped vertically into the loose material before compaction. Mark the target depth on a stake or board placed at the edge of the area. Check depth at multiple points across the project since ground level is rarely perfectly flat.

Should I add extra depth for compaction?

Yes. If you need a finished depth of 4 inches after compaction, spread the gravel to approximately 4.5-5 inches loose before compacting. This accounts for the 10-20% settlement that occurs during rolling or tamping.
